How do robot policies
use language?

We tested whether vision-language-action models follow the meaning of an instruction, remain responsive to language during execution, and combine familiar objects with new destinations.

The starting observation

A robot can pick up the correct object and still fail after an instruction is reworded. A final success score does not tell us whether the problem arose before the grasp, during transport, or when choosing where to place the object.

Wording
Some words redirect behavior

Particular destination words cause failures after a correct grasp. Unusable destination words can produce the same behavior as no instruction.

Timing
Responsiveness has a window

A held bowl can still be redirected early in transport. This becomes much harder later; bottle tasks show earlier and stronger constraints.

Generalization
Familiar skills do not always combine

Two Goal-only models score 0/100 on five new object–destination combinations, despite high success on standard tasks.

The next research question

What makes physical knowledge transferable from vision-language models to robotic control?

Background · What is being tested?
02 / 12

The scene is familiar. The language is what we change.

A VLM processes images and language. A VLA policy uses visual observations and an instruction to produce robot actions. Here, an episode is a complete simulated attempt at a task.

Environment

LIBERO-Goal contains 10 manipulation tasks in a shared scene setting, with a bowl, cream cheese, a wine bottle, a plate, a cabinet, a stove, and a wine rack. Familiar goals include placing the bowl on the plate or on the cabinet.

Sharing a scene does not make every episode physically identical. The controlled branching experiment explicitly restores the same intermediate state before changing the instruction.

Method · How do we locate a language failure?
03 / 12

Save one grasp state, then test different continuations

Question

If a paraphrased instruction leads to a failed placement, did it already cause a bad grasp, or did the text affect the policy after the grasp? Comparing two complete episodes cannot separate these possibilities because their trajectories may differ from the beginning.

1 · COLLECT
Reach a stable grasp

Run the policy with an original instruction or a selected paraphrase.

→
2 · SNAPSHOT
Save the full state

Preserve the robot, objects, fixture positions, and gripper state.

→
3 · BRANCH
Restore and change text

Start each continuation from that same snapshot. Clear queued actions.

→
4 · EXECUTE
Observe the outcome

Let the robot act with visual feedback; check which goal it completes.

The original/paraphrase comparison uses both the instruction before the grasp and the instruction after it.
Instruction used to reach the graspContinue with original wordingContinue with paraphrased wording
Original wordingReference continuation.If this fails, changing text after the grasp can cause a failure from an otherwise usable state.
Paraphrased wordingIf this succeeds, that grasp state can still complete the intended task with the original wording.Measures continued behavior under the paraphrase.
CONCRETE EXAMPLE

The robot is already holding the bowl. Restore that exact state twice: once continue with “put the bowl on top of the cabinet,” and once with “put the bowl on top of the locker.” Any resulting difference cannot be explained by the two branches having picked up different objects.

What this establishes. The intervention can locate a behavioral effect of language after a particular state. It does not, by itself, identify which internal representation or neural module caused the effect.

Sources: [1] E3; [3] restoration fixes. Early runs reproduced outcomes but had a few non-identical trajectories. Later restoration checks were tightened; π0.5 and StarVLA each passed 12 same-state, same-instruction repeats with identical actions under controlled randomness.

Experiment 1 · What kinds of wording matter?
04 / 12

Most tested changes were harmless after grasping; some were not

What we did

At saved grasp states, we compared original instructions, LIBERO-Para paraphrases, and changes to function words. Function words are grammatical words such as articles; content words name the action, object, or destination. These tests ask whether failure is a generic response to any textual change.

Broad result: high continuation success

The reported OFT original comparison has n = 140; its paraphrase/control groups each have n = 420. The selected phrases below are specific cases, not the average of this table.

Specific result: a destination synonym changes behavior

put the bowl on top of the locker

Starting from original-instruction grasp states, OFT placed the bowl on the cabinet in 4/20 branches and on the stove in 16/20.

In the complementary test, grasp states collected using this paraphrase could complete the cabinet task in 15/20 continuations when the original wording was restored.

How to interpret the combination of results

A blanket claim that “any wording change breaks control” does not explain the near-identical aggregate rates for original text and function-word changes. The locker example shows that particular content-word changes can still affect destination selection after grasping. Successful rescue with the original wording shows that many paraphrase-sourced grasps remain usable.

Scope: the first selected-phrase test used one source paraphrase per task. With three source paraphrases per task, failures still concentrated in bowl tasks, while overall continuation success was 92% for both models. The finding is about particular tasks and phrases, not all paraphrases.

Related E1 result: shuffling instruction words preserved 84% original-task success; empty text gave 7%, and another task’s instruction gave 0%. That last score checks the original goal, so it does not tell us whether the newly requested task was followed. The 97.4% E0 reference is a separate evaluation batch.

Source: [1] E1, F1–F2, selected source phrases, and the three-source-paraphrase follow-up.

Experiment 2 · What happens when the destination word is unusable?
05 / 12

An unfamiliar word can behave like an empty instruction

Question and procedure

Does an unrecognized destination make the policy choose arbitrarily, continue its original plan, or fall back to a recurring destination? We collected original-instruction grasp states, then replaced the destination noun with invented words such as blicket and dax. We compared these branches with empty text and with the original instruction.

Result and explanation

The complete measured outcome distributions for invented destination words and empty text matched: total variation distance = 0.00. This distance measures how different two outcome distributions are; zero means no difference in the observed distributions. For OFT, the stove is a recurring fallback destination in these held-bowl states.

Changing the object noun to an invented word after the bowl had already been grasped preserved the intended goal in all 10 states of each task. At that stage, object identity is already established physically, while the destination can still need disambiguation.

WHY SUCCESS ALONE CAN MISLEAD

If the intended destination is already the stove, OFT can succeed even with empty text. Likewise, VLA-Adapter’s 20/20 success on the selected “locker” phrase is compatible with its tendency to place the bowl on the cabinet. Success alone does not distinguish understanding from a matching fallback.

THE SECOND MODEL IS DIFFERENT

VLA-Adapter sometimes goes to the cabinet and sometimes continues toward the previous destination. For the plate task, “dax” kept the plate destination in 10/10 cases. Its behavior does not satisfy all of the predefined default-destination predictions.

Conclusion. The OFT observations support a destination-key/fallback account in this setting. This is a behavioral explanation; we have not identified an internal lookup table or proved that it explains every failure. The follow-up hypothesis H3′ is supported for OFT and not fully supported for VLA-Adapter.

Source: [1] H3′ invented-word follow-up, F5, and selected source phrases. All “default” statements here refer to the tested intermediate states.

Experiment 3 · When can language still change the goal?
06 / 12

A bowl remains redirectable early after grasping

What we did and what the numbers mean

Run an original task, save states at different times, and branch to another destination for the same object. For example, switch from “bowl on the plate” to “bowl on the stove.” The redirection rate f is the proportion completing the newly requested goal.

To align tasks of different lengths, q measures elapsed steps from stable grasp to completion of the original goal. Thus q = 25% means one quarter of that time interval, not one quarter of the spatial distance. A same-state continuation with the original instruction checks that restarting still works.

How to read this result

For VLA-Adapter, changing the instruction at q = 25% still produces the new bowl destination in 53% of the evaluated branches. By q = 50%, that rate is 7%. Original-instruction bowl continuations succeed in 97–100% across these times, so the decline is not explained by restarting alone.

The tested bottle branches show no redirection in this phase-aligned batch. An earlier timing experiment also found zero redirection 20 steps before the grasp. This supports an earlier constraint on bottle redirection, rather than a universal moment at which every task stops responding to language.

Conclusion. Language can affect the goal after grasping, but the available window depends on the task and state. Under the predefined H1″ rule, VLA-Adapter is supported; OFT’s 28% misses the predicted ≥50% at q = 25%, but does not trigger the ≤20% refutation threshold.

* Bottle original-instruction restarts at q = 75% succeed only 55–57%, so that late point is inconclusive. Earlier E3 snapshots allowed bottle cabinet-to-rack redirection in 13/40 OFT and 8/40 VLA-Adapter branches; the reverse direction was 0/40. The E4 zeros therefore should not be generalized to every bottle state or direction.

Sources: [1] F4, E4 and H1″; [2] §3.3. A discarded timing choice and the distinction between observed responsiveness and its mechanism are explained in Sections 10 and 12.

Experiment 4 · Can we predict a wording failure cheaply?
07 / 12

An offline action difference predicts paraphrase failures

What we did

Hold the initial visual observation fixed. Run the policy with the original instruction, a paraphrase, and the other Goal-task instructions. Compare the predicted action chunks, which are short sequences of robot commands. No robot execution is needed to compute this score.

R_action = action-chunk distance between original and paraphrase
÷ average action-chunk distance between original and the other task instructions.

Near 0 means the paraphrase barely changes the output. Near 1 means it changes the output as much as a typical different task. The score can exceed 1; it is not a probability. Original and other-task outputs can be reused across paraphrases.

Join scores to recorded LIBERO-Para rollout outcomes: 3,889 matched paraphrases per model.

What does AUC = 0.866 mean?

Choose a failed paraphrase and a successful paraphrase at random. The score ranks the failed one as riskier about 86.6% of the time, counting ties as half. It does not mean the robot succeeds 86.6% of the time, or that a classification threshold has 86.6% accuracy.

As R_action increases from its lowest fifth to its highest fifth, recorded success falls from 84.5% to 14.1% for VLA-Adapter and from 93.6% to 14.5% for OFT.

Conclusion. A fixed-observation output comparison is a useful candidate for screening language fragility. It measures predictive association, not semantic understanding. On the same resampled paraphrases, R_action beats keyword similarity by +0.039 [+0.022, +0.058] AUC for VLA-Adapter and +0.096 [+0.082, +0.110] for OFT (paired bootstrap, 2,000 resamples). Validation on different starting states remains to be done.

Sources: [1] E6 → LIBERO-Para; [3] score and AUC implementations. Scores are averaged over available E6 initial observations; LIBERO-Para has one rollout per paraphrase from init 0. This shared early-state setting limits generalization of the reported AUC.

Similar totals conceal different abilities

π0.5 and StarVLA total 49/180 and 46/180. Yet cream cheese → cabinet top is 90% versus 5%, while opening the top drawer is 0% versus 85%. Their combination performance is strongly goal-dependent.

π0.5 opens the top drawer during the longer cheese instruction but fails the standalone drawer instruction. This suggests context dependence. Since different goal evaluations can start from different fixture layouts, a matched-state test is needed to isolate the wording effect.

The predefined retrieval prediction did not pass

The prediction was that at least half of failures would complete a familiar task sharing the requested object or destination. Using all failures as the denominator gives:

VLA-Adapter78/180 = 43.3%
OFT81/168 = 48.2%

OFT has 168 failures because 12 of 180 episodes succeeded. Drawer-only goals were not fully specified by the original classification rule. Post-hoc alternative definitions exceed 50%, but are not used to change the verdict.

Conclusion. Familiar-task substitution is an important observed failure pattern, but the predefined claim that it explains a majority of failures was not met. The separate prediction that Goal-only models score ≤30% on the new-goal set was met.

Interpretation · What may explain the observations?
10 / 12

Data coverage, upstream knowledge, and transfer are separate factors

The experiments establish behavioral effects. They motivate the following explanations, which still need controlled training comparisons.

1. Narrow data can make language distinctions unnecessary

If similar states in the training set nearly always lead to the same action, the policy may have little incentive to use language there. Conversely, examples that share a state but require different outcomes may encourage instruction use. This is a candidate explanation for the task-dependent redirection window; we have not yet measured or manipulated those local data branches.

2. Upstream physical knowledge may be missing, lost, or difficult to use

A policy that fails a new object–destination combination may lack useful spatial knowledge, may lose that knowledge during robot fine-tuning, or may preserve it without obtaining a control benefit. The current checkpoint comparisons do not distinguish these cases. “Fine-tuning erased the ability” requires measurements before and after fine-tuning.

3. A task can become difficult to redirect because of its physical state

Different destinations may require different grasp poses or earlier preparatory actions. Once the robot takes an incompatible approach, a new instruction may require recovery behavior absent from the demonstrations. Failure to redirect therefore measures behavioral responsiveness; it does not prove that the network stopped processing language.

EXISTING EVIDENCE THAT TRAINING COVERAGE MATTERS

For the same OFT architecture, moving from Goal-only to four-suite training changes R_action for object-word paraphrases from 0.297 to 0.454 and for empty text from 1.003 to 1.494. This associates training coverage with different language sensitivity. Greater sensitivity is not automatically better understanding, and the normalization denominator can also change.

The research opportunity. Test which forms of spatial or physical supervision make an upstream representation more useful for control under the same downstream robot-data budget. Language can express reusable relations, action conditions, and effects; whether learning these descriptions improves action must be measured.

Sources: [1] E6 data comparison; [2] §11; research discussion. The three explanations above are hypotheses, not additional experimental findings.

Teach spatial knowledge, then test whether it helps action

Planned procedure

Start from Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct. Train the VLM on simulator-derived question–answer pairs, then use its VLM weights to initialize StarVLA-OFT. Train each branch with the same LIBERO-Goal demonstrations and action-head design. Finally, evaluate familiar tasks and held-out new goal combinations.

WHAT THE CURRENT QA TEACHES

Questions concern image positions, relative locations, distances, what the gripper holds, what supports an object, and drawer/stove states. For example: “What is the gripper holding?” → “The bowl,” when supported by the simulator state. The current “next step” label is a simple rule; it does not yet teach action consequences or physical explanations.

Learned?

QA training

Does held-out QA accuracy improve? Without this improvement, the intended upstream capability has not been demonstrated.

Starting point measured: the raw VLM beats a constant-answer floor on no fact (left/right 0.85 vs 0.87; what the gripper holds 0.15 vs 0.74; supports 0.45 vs 0.92; distances 0.12–0.24 vs 0.46–0.48). A format hint and 2× upscaling do not fix it. Yet linear probes read the held object (0.95) and object and gripper positions to about 1 cm from its hidden states. After VLA fine-tuning (StarVLA 4in1) its text output is degenerate, while the probes read slightly better. Caveat: a 32×32 pixel probe already reaches 0.87–0.98 here.

Preserved?

VLA fine-tuning

Does the QA gain remain? A decline would support loss of measured capability during this training stage.

Useful?

Robot evaluation

Does new-goal success improve without degrading familiar tasks? Retained QA alone does not demonstrate transfer.

Evidence boundaries · Discussion and references
12 / 12

What we can report now, and what remains open

Supported by the current observations

  • Specific wording changes can alter behavior after a correct grasp.
  • OFT’s invented destination words match empty-text outcomes in the tested bowl states.
  • Mid-execution redirection depends on progress, object, and task pair.
  • Initial action sensitivity predicts paraphrase failures in the evaluated setting.
  • High familiar-task success coexists with poor or uneven new-goal performance.

Not yet established

  • That all failures come from noun-based trajectory retrieval.
  • That stronger VLM pretraining alone explains the differences between models.
  • That robot fine-tuning erased previously demonstrated physical knowledge.
  • That spatial QA training improves downstream control.
  • That every zero-success new goal is feasible under the evaluation protocol.
Why one earlier timing hypothesis was rejected: the original follow-up H1′ predicted continued bowl redirection at a fixed 20 steps after grasping. Both models reached 0%, triggering its refutation rule. Inspection showed that some original goals were already achieved or nearly complete at that point. The verdict was retained; H1″ was specified separately using phase-relative timing. The two batches used different scene samples and cannot be paired episode by episode.
Discussion with the advisor: the next decision is which upstream knowledge to prioritize: static spatial relations first, followed by action conditions and consequences if justified. The intended contribution is an evidence-based account of what knowledge transfers to control and under which training conditions.
